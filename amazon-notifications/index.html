01: The Problem

Engage's notification patterns failed accessibility standards and buried the information HR leaders needed most

Engage is Amazon's internal tool for helping HR leaders connect with and develop their direct reports. It runs in more than 200 fulfillment centers worldwide, so any problem in the tool was never a small, contained bug. It touched HR leaders everywhere, every day.

The existing notification patterns, on-site, off-site, warning, and task complete, did not meet international accessibility standards. Several of them signaled status and urgency with color alone: a colored dot, a colored icon, and nothing else to back it up.

That choice blocked critical information. A leader who could not distinguish the colors, or who was scanning quickly across a table of dozens of direct reports, had no second way to tell which associates needed urgent attention and which did not. The patterns that were supposed to help HR leaders stay on top of their teams were instead causing confusion.

02: My Role And How I Worked

I owned the redesign of every notification pattern in Engage, end to end

I audited the existing notification patterns across Engage, researched how other platforms solved the same problem, and redesigned the on-site, off-site, warning, and task complete patterns to meet international accessibility standards without losing any of the information they were meant to carry.

04: The Decisions

Two changes that made the patterns accessible without losing any information

Each of these two changes was really a cluster of smaller decisions, not a single fix.

Decision 01

Move status onto the avatar, fix the contrast, and turn a solid fill into an open ring

The old presence badge paired a green dot for on premise with a gray dot for off premise, both sitting directly on a white background. Rounds of review and testing surfaced four separate problems with it:

  1. 1 Contrast. Both the green and the gray badge colors fell below accessibility contrast standards against the white background. I corrected both.
  2. 2 Space. I moved the badge off the name and onto the avatar itself, as a consistent corner icon. That freed up the width the name needed, so longer employee names fit in the card without wrapping or truncating.
  3. 3 Shape, not just color. In usability testing, a solid gray dot next to a solid green dot still read as ambiguous, since both simply looked "filled," just in different colors. After two rounds of testing, I replaced the solid gray fill with an open ring, a gray outline with an empty center, so managers could tell on premise from off premise by shape as well as color, at a glance instead of on close inspection.
  4. 4 Warmth. I also kept every avatar circular rather than square. Contour bias research (Bar & Neta, 2006) shows people read sharp, angular contours as threat cues and rounded contours as safer — the right association for a tool centered on an employee's wellbeing.
Decision 02

Give urgent warnings their own spot instead of sharing one with status

In the old design, a warning icon sometimes took over the exact spot normally used for the online status dot. A leader could see someone was at risk, or that they were online, but never both at once, because the two signals were fighting for the same pixel. I gave warnings a dedicated position, a small flag in the corner of the card, so status and urgency can both be visible at the same time.

05: The Solution

The same patterns, used consistently across three surfaces

Once the patterns were redesigned, I applied them everywhere Engage already used status and risk signals, rather than fixing them only in the place that prompted the project.

  1. 1 My engagement list (Home page): The cards leaders check first now carry the avatar status badge and, where relevant, a separate corner flag for schedule risk, so both signals are visible without crowding each other.
  2. 2 Employee detail page: The standalone "On premise" text line is gone. Presence now lives as the same badge used everywhere else, and a flagged risk surfaces as a page level warning with a link to see details, worded rather than only colored.
  3. 3 Employee table (Home page): Rows with a schedule risk get a light background tint in addition to the icon and the words "Schedule risk," so the row still reads clearly if a leader is scanning quickly or using a screen magnifier.

07: What I Learned

Clarity and over communication mattered more than any single screen

This project touched systems and teams beyond Engage itself, and that scale is what shaped what I took away from it.

Over communicate when many systems and teams are involved
Clarity and over communication were crucial on this project specifically because the changes touched multiple systems and teams at once. A fix that looked simple on one screen had ripple effects everywhere else the same pattern was reused, and keeping every team aligned on that took more repetition than I expected.
Learn to communicate value so others advocate for it
Getting this proposed into Amazon's Worldwide Org design library meant the work had to be understood by people who never used Engage directly. I learned strategies for communicating the value of a decision clearly enough that other people could advocate for it in rooms I was not in.
