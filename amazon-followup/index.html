01: The Problem

HR leaders were writing followup reminders on paper and still missing them

HR leaders within Amazon's worldwide operations (WWO) were required to complete suggested tasks inside Engage, an internal tool for connecting and developing relationships with direct reports. But there was no structure inside Engage for creating followups after an initial meeting.

Leaders were writing down who to follow up with on paper. With 30 to 40 direct reports per shift, notes got missed. Some leaders reported forgetting to follow up entirely because of the volume of conversations they were managing.

The problem was not lack of intent. It was lack of infrastructure. Every reminder lived outside the system that was supposed to support the work.

"I speak to 30 to 40 direct reports every shift. It's really difficult to write down notes and remember to follow up with them again."

Interview participant, HR Leader

"My team is my family and I want to be able to provide them all the resources they need, but sometimes it can be overwhelming due to the short amount of time."

Interview participant, HR Leader

03: Research

I interviewed 30 HR leaders across North America, the EU, and Asia to understand exactly where the process broke down

A number of HR leaders had surfaced a feature request to the product manager. They needed a way to create followups inside Engage. The PM asked our UX team to examine what was really happening before we designed anything.

I requested a participant list from the PM that included both regular users and super users, defined as leaders who had logged more than 10,000 engagements. I interviewed 10 leaders from each region: North America, the European Union, and Asia, 30 participants in total, including some of the leaders who had originally surfaced the request. During the interviews I asked behavioral and task oriented questions: how they currently manage followups, how often they check in with direct reports, what topics those conversations cover, and where things fall through the cracks.

04: How I Ran It

Six design sprints, from research to tested prototypes

I moved from stakeholder alignment through research, design, and testing across six design sprints.

01
Feature request and scoping
PM surfaced HR leader requests. I aligned with stakeholders on research scope and recruited 10 participants across NA and the EU, including super users with 10,000+ engagements.
02
Interviews and synthesis
Conducted 10 user interviews. Synthesized into a findings report with patterns and a hypothesis. Presented to stakeholders before moving to design.
03

Developed several design iterations using the Org design library. Synced with engineering to validate technical feasibility. Defined five user flows covering create, edit, delete, complete, and error states.
04

Built InVision prototypes and recruited 10 new participants for usability testing. Defined task metrics for success and fail rates. All 10 participants completed every task.
05

Created a stakeholder presentation and published an internal Amazon Wiki page explaining the feature for customers and the wider team.

05: The Solution

A notification and reminder system built into Engage so followups never live outside the tool

The Followup feature lets HR leaders set reminders while logging previously completed tasks with direct reports. Leaders can schedule, edit, delete, and complete followups without leaving Engage, and continue existing conversations from the same place they were started.

I designed the feature across three existing pages in Engage: the home page, the employee detail page, and the log engagement page. Followups surface in each context depending on where the leader is in their workflow.

  1. 1 Home page: Leaders toggle followup reminders on and off directly from the home page, so pending actions are always one tap away.
  2. 2 Employee Detail page: Every followup action a leader takes with a direct report is logged automatically in the engagement history table, creating a clear record without any extra steps.
  3. 3 Edit followup modal (home page): Leaders can reschedule or adjust a followup from the home page to fit their availability, without losing the context of the original conversation.
  4. 4 Recommended engagement card (Log engagement page): When a followup reminder is due, it surfaces as a card on the log engagement page so leaders can act on it in the same flow they use every day.
  5. 5 Recommended engagement card (Employee Detail page): Pending followup actions for a specific employee are surfaced directly on their detail page, so leaders have the full picture before starting a conversation.
  6. 6 Log engagement page: Leaders create followup reminders from inside the log engagement flow, so the reminder is set at the moment the conversation is logged rather than as a separate task afterwards.

06: User Flows

I mapped five flows covering every state a leader could encounter with a followup

The flows are written around Hannah, an Area Manager, and her direct report Regina. Each flow tests a distinct interaction: the happy path, the error state, completion, editing, and deletion.

1
Create a followup
Hannah had a conversation with Regina about medical leave and creates a followup as a reminder for herself.
2
Unable to create
Hannah must complete an existing followup before creating a new one. This covers the error state.
3
Complete a followup
Hannah completes a followup by logging her engagement with Regina from the reminder.
4
Edit a followup
Hannah reschedules a followup due the next day to the following week using the edit modal.
5
Delete a followup
Hannah deletes the followup after the issue with Regina was resolved on its own.

08: What I Learned

The first launch answered whether leaders would use it. The next question is whether it changes team outcomes.

What we do not yet know is whether more consistent followup translates to measurable improvements in team morale or retention. That is the next phase of this work, not a finished one.

Research surfaced the feature before design started
The project started as a feature request from a small number of leaders. Research confirmed it was a universal problem across the user base. Going broad with recruitment before narrowing to design was the right call and something I would repeat.
Conduct a second round of usability testing with seasoned users
The first round tested whether the feature worked. The next round should test whether it works efficiently for leaders who have been using it for several months and have formed habits around it.
Run field studies to assess impact on team morale
Usage data shows leaders are creating followups. Field studies would tell us whether the quality of those followup conversations is improving and whether direct reports feel the difference.
Build a recurring followup notification
Several interview participants mentioned wanting Engage to suggest followup topics proactively. A recurring notification feature would build on the foundation this design established and further reduce the cognitive load on leaders managing large teams.
