Search Widget Redesign

01: The Problem

Search volume for Hotel Only search and Dynamic Packages landed under projection, and no one could point to a specific cause

The widget before the redesign: dark background, new product types buried under a secondary tab

Costco Travel had just added Hotel Only search and Dynamic Packages alongside our long-standing Vacation Packages. Both new lines underperformed against the business case from launch.

  1. Vacation Packages had their own dedicated tab in the widget.
  2. Dynamic Packages covered three combinations: Hotel + Flight, Hotel + Flight + Car, and Hotel + Car. Not every destination available under Vacation Packages was available under Dynamic Packages.

The widget had been built and patched around Vacation Packages for years, so Hotel Only search and Dynamic Packages were slotted in as secondary tabs instead of designed in from scratch. It looked like one unified product. It was actually optimized for Vacation Packages, at the expense of the other two.

The initial ask I received was to make the new tabs more visible. I pushed back, because a visibility fix assumes layout is the whole problem. I started with research instead, and that changed the scope of the work.

"I don't even know what the difference is between these two package things. I'll just call an agent if I want the good deal."

Usability session participant, on the Vacation Package vs. dynamic package distinction

02: My Role And How I Worked

I owned every decision on the widget, and built the process that made testing repeatable

  • What I owned Every design decision on the widget, from concept through the A/B test spec. I partnered with our researcher on study design and sat in on every usability session myself instead of reading a summary afterward.
  • What I built The A/B testing worksheet that became our standard process: hypothesis, the specific user problem it addressed, the metric it should move, and the metric it must not move. Every test after the first used this template.
  • Who I worked with I partnered with one researcher on study design, two engineers who built and instrumented each variant, and a product manager who owned stakeholder roadmap conversations. I ran a weekly sync to keep the four of us aligned.
10 to 20%
of the time, users preferred our widget over Expedia, Booking.com, and Google Travel in comparative usability sessions. That number is what got stakeholders to agree this was a design problem, not a marketing problem.
03: How I Ran It
01
Research
Competitive usability studies against three competitors, plus a stakeholder workshop to align on one shared success metric before any design work started.
02
Concept
Explored three directions: spinning Vacation Packages into its own widget, a single search field, and a left-hand vertical navigation. Tested all three in low-fidelity usability sessions.
03
A/B test
Every validated concept went into a live test in Google Optimize before it shipped broadly. No decision above a cosmetic tweak skipped this step.
04
Ship & re-baseline
Winning variants became the new control. The next test always measured against the most recent shipped state, not the original widget, so gains compounded instead of resetting.
04: Decisions I Made, And What Each One Cost

Four shipped iterations. Each one answered a specific finding from research

Decision 01

Moved new products into the primary tab position

Stakeholders were most nervous about this one. It meant demoting Vacation Packages, our highest-revenue line, out of the default position. I walked them through the A/B testing worksheet and the cannibalization risk math before I got sign-off to test it live.

+5.8% new product search, legacy still gained 1.6%
Decision 02

Collapsed Hotel Only search into a single line

Every competitor I studied used a single-line hotel search. Ours had multiple stacked fields left over from an older layout. This was the simplest fix in the project.

+3.8% hotel search
Decision 03

Combined Vacation Package and Dynamic Package search into one entry point

Vacation Packages are negotiated hotel by hotel by our buyers, with exclusive extras but limited destination coverage. Dynamic packages are negotiated at the chain level and available almost everywhere. Users could not tell the two apart, and the pill-box selector asking them to pick one upfront was asking them to answer a question they didn't understand.

After dozens of studies and design iterations, I removed the pill boxes entirely and consolidated both products into a single search field, routing users to the right result based on the destination they typed. It's the same pattern that had already worked for Hotel Only search, so I wasn't starting from zero on trust in the approach. New package searches went up. Vacation Packages dipped for two weeks, a tradeoff I flagged to stakeholders going in.

+7.2% new package searches, Vacation Packages dipped 0.4% for two weeks
Decision 04

Added a curated destination spotlight instead of bringing the selector back

The 0.4% dip on Vacation Packages needed a fix that didn't undo decision 03. Rather than reintroduce the pill boxes, I designed a graphical dropdown that surfaces top-value curated destinations the moment a user focuses the search field, while still letting them type any destination they want. It recovered Vacation Packages without touching the routing win.

Vacation Packages recovered to +2.1%, new package gains held at +7.2%
05: Managing Stakeholders Through This

Not every stakeholder trusted the same evidence, so I stopped presenting one kind

Some stakeholders moved on a single compelling usability clip. Others wanted nothing but the Google Optimize confidence interval. Early on I built one deck for every audience. It landed weakly with everyone, and that was my mistake to fix. I split the readout into a qualitative highlight reel and a quantitative results summary, and let each stakeholder group pull from whichever convinced them.

I set the pace deliberately slow: research finding, targeted design, usability check, then an A/B test before anything shipped broadly. Some stakeholders wanted the new-product problem solved in one redesign, and that frustrated them. I held the line, because the alternative was shipping changes nobody could attribute results to, which is how the original widget ended up unmeasured in the first place.

A gap I'll name directly

The Vacation Package vs. dynamic distinction is still not solved

The destination spotlight recovered the metric, but it didn't resolve the underlying confusion users have about what separates a Vacation Package from a dynamic one. It's a mitigation, not a fix. That distinction needs dedicated design and content work I haven't had the roadmap space to do yet.

07: What I Learned
Bring stakeholders into the research, not just the results
Some stakeholders had never worked with a designer who tested hypotheses this rigorously. Showing them the research, not only the outcome, is what got decision 03 approved. Removing the pill-box selector entirely was the biggest structural gamble I made in this project.
One reusable testing worksheet beat any single test result
The worksheet format, hypothesis, user problem, metric to move, metric to protect, let the team run test after test without relitigating methodology each time. It outlived this project.
