01: The Problem

An outside vendor's style guide left too much open to interpretation, and accessibility was never part of it

Costco Travel's style guide had been built years earlier by an external vendor. It covered the basics loosely and never addressed accessibility at all. Every designer filled the gaps with their own judgment, so product, brand, and engineering had each drifted into a different visual language without meaning to.

I ran a full UI audit before proposing anything: colors, typography, buttons, forms, icons, dropdowns, cataloged across every live page. I found more than 40 components with three or more inconsistent variants in production at the same time, across four product teams. The audit is what turned "the site feels inconsistent" into a specific, defensible case.

It's also the part of this project I'd do differently if I started over. I audited product surfaces one at a time as I got to them, rather than scoping the full inventory up front, which meant I found new drift late into the project that a wider first pass would have caught sooner.

02: My Role And How I Worked

  • What I owned The atomic design architecture, the four product principles, component naming conventions, and final review on every component before it shipped to the library.
  • What I delegated Component production in Figma and documentation authoring in The Forge, both owned by the design team with my review before anything reached engineering or marketing.
  • Who I worked with Three other designers, one researcher, and one developer who built the Figma to Forge sync plugin. Our team had people who'd previously worked at Microsoft, Adobe, and Google, and I leaned on that range deliberately when I assigned component ownership.

04: Decisions I Made, And What Each One Cost

Three decisions that shaped how the system actually got adopted

Decision 01

Built accessibility into every component, not as a separate pass

I required every component to clear WCAG AA contrast and spacing before it could ship, rather than auditing for accessibility after the library existed. That meant slower initial output. It also meant zero components needed accessibility rework later, which is where most systems I benchmarked were still losing time.

Zero accessibility rework across the shipped library
Decision 02

Built the code library alongside the Figma library, not after it

I pushed for The Forge and the developer code library to launch the same week as the Figma component library, not months later. Engineering had a documented, working pattern to reference from day one instead of reverse engineering our Figma files.

Decision 03

Gave marketing a standing sign off checkpoint instead of a one time review

Early drafts of new patterns went to marketing once, at the end. I changed that to a biweekly sign off after brand flagged two components post launch that didn't match voice guidelines. The fix cost us a slower cadence. It also meant nothing shipped that brand had to walk back in front of stakeholders.

05: Documentation And Governance

A design system with no documentation is a system that forks the moment someone needs something it doesn't have

I built The Forge as the single source of truth: every component's description, construction rules, states, behavior, accessibility notes, and real usage examples in one place, kept in sync with Figma through a plugin our developer built. Engineers read the same documentation instead of asking design to clarify spacing in Slack.

I ran stakeholder buy in on three different cadences, because one size did not fit all of them. Executives saw a single presentation on pain points and projected company wide impact. Engineering got weekly working sessions where they could push back on a pattern before it shipped. Marketing got the biweekly sign off I introduced after the first post launch flags. Running three cadences at once was more coordination overhead than I expected going in, but it's what got all three groups to a real sense of shared ownership rather than a mandate handed down to them.

07: What I Learned
Accessibility has to be a requirement, not a review step
Requiring WCAG AA before a component could ship cost time upfront. It's the reason the library has needed zero accessibility rework since launch. Treating it as a later cleanup pass is the more expensive path, even though it looks faster at first.
A system earns adoption through documentation, not a mandate
Engineering stopped asking design about styling once The Forge gave them a real reference. That happened because documentation shipped with the components, not after them. A library without documentation is a library people work around.
This is an ongoing effort

Templates and full pages are still in progress, and component coverage keeps expanding as new product surfaces come online. I'm still running the weekly engineering sessions and the biweekly marketing sign off, and I expect the next major push to be page level templates for the highest traffic booking flows.
